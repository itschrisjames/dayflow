Every task has a five-minute button

One tap starts a timer. No scheduling first, no category, no estimate. The timer runs past five minutes rather than shouting at you, and it records how long the thing actually took — so next time DayFlow fills in the real number instead of your optimistic one.

Built for the specific ways this goes wrong

A missed day doesn't reset your streak

One free miss a week. Every habit also shows how many of the last 30 days you turned up, so a bad Tuesday can't erase the month.

Focus 3

One tap hides everything except three things. A forty-item inbox is the thing that makes people close the app.

Nothing goes stale in your face

Anything untouched for three weeks steps aside into Someday on its own. Nothing is deleted, and nothing nags.

Warnings before, not at

Five minutes before a block starts and five before it ends — being yanked out of something at the exact moment never works.

Momentum

Finish one thing and the next is offered with a ten-second countdown and an easy out. That gap is where the hour goes.

Undo, always

A labelled undo bar sits within thumb reach for twelve seconds after anything destructive. Impulsive taps are part of the territory.

Install it in twenty seconds

DayFlow is a web app, so there's no App Store and no download. Adding it to your Home Screen makes it behave like any other app — full screen, works offline, its own icon.

  1. Open this address in Safari on your iPhone (Chrome on Android): https://itschrisjames.github.io/dayflow/
  2. Tap the Share button at the bottom of Safari.
  3. Scroll down and tap Add to Home Screen, then Add.
  4. Open it from your Home Screen from now on — notifications and offline use only work from there, not from a Safari tab.

What it doesn't do

Worth knowing before you rely on it. I'd rather you find these here than three weeks in.

  • Your data lives on your phone only. No account, no server, nothing to leak — but clearing Safari's website data deletes it. DayFlow nags you to export a backup for exactly this reason.
  • Reminders only appear while it's open, unless you run the small notification server included in the repo. iOS gives a web app no way to wake itself up.
  • Clock alarms need a Shortcut you build once, because iOS has no alarm API. Calendar alerts work with no setup at all.
  • The assistant is a pattern matcher running on your phone, not a language model. It understands a fixed set of phrasings and nothing you type reaches the internet.
  • Google Calendar sync needs your own credential. Two-way sync works, but since there's no server, you supply a Google client ID — about three minutes, explained in the app.
